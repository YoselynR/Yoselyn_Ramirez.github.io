Building the Industrial Energy Efficiency Data Explorer

Capstone Team: Eva Newby, Oksana Protskha, Naomi Moraes, and Yos Ramirez

Introduction

Industrial energy use sits at the heart of a big challenge:
Powering the modern world without overheating the planet.

Factories produce the clothes we wear, the food we eat, the bikes we ride — and they also account for about 30% of U.S. greenhouse gas emissions, according to the EPA.

Our capstone team set out to support the people working to address that challenge: industrial energy analysts and modelers.

Our project, advised by Dr. Eric Masanet at the Bren School and in partnership with the Industrial Sustainability Analysis Lab, resulted in a tool we call the Industrial Energy Efficiency Data Explorer. It’s designed to make data-driven policy and technology analysis simpler, faster, and more reproducible.

Why Industrial Efficiency Matters

Manufacturing is a double-edged sword.
On one hand, it’s essential to our quality of life and economic vitality. On the other, it’s an enormous energy consumer and polluter.

Take a closer look:

  • Industrial activity emits carbon dioxide (CO₂), nitrogen oxides (NOₓ), and sulfur dioxide (SO₂).
  • These emissions impact climate, public health, and air quality.

The good news? Analysts, researchers, and policy experts are actively working to improve efficiency and reduce emissions by building models that assess how manufacturing uses energy and where improvements can be made.

But for these experts to make progress, they need high-quality, accessible data — and that’s where our project comes in.

A Use Case

Meet Erin

To ground the problem in a real-world context, let’s consider a user like Erin, an energy analyst working for the state of California.

Erin’s role is to help policymakers understand how to improve energy efficiency in industrial plants. She’s just been asked to investigate the case for supporting motor efficiency upgrades in California’s food processing industry — a request inspired by a paper showing these upgrades cut 187 million kg of CO₂ annually across the U.S., equivalent to the emissions from 44,000 cars.

Her boss wants to know:

  1. How much energy and emissions could be saved by applying this in California?
  2. What would it cost to implement?
  3. Should the state create an incentive program?

The Challenge

Fragmented, Manual Data Workflows

To answer these questions, Erin turns to the Industrial Assessment Centers (IAC) database — the world’s largest dataset on industrial energy audits, with over 20,000 audits and 160,000 recommendations collected over 40+ years.

But there’s a problem:

  • The data lacks emissions factors
  • Doesn’t account for changing electricity prices
  • Must be manually cleaned, integrated, and analyzed — mostly in Excel

Each time new data is released, Erin has to redo this painstaking process from scratch.

Our Capstone Goal

Our team set out to simplify and modernize this process

Objective
Build a user-friendly tool that enables energy analysts to quickly explore industrial energy efficiency data, draw insights, and inform decisions about decarbonization pathways.

What We Delivered

  1. An integrated dataset, combining audits, emissions, and economic data
  2. A data update pipeline, built for scalability and minimal manual effort
  3. An interactive dashboard, so analysts like Erin can filter, visualize, and explore data instantly

Under the Hood

How It Works

Streamlined Process
We designed the workflow to minimize friction:

  • Data download remains manual (to avoid fragile automation)
  • A single script handles cleaning, formatting, calculations, and merging
  • Analysts can now update the full dataset in under 30 minutes

Dashboard Design

Built using Dash (Python) — an open-source framework chosen for its long-term maintainability and alignment with our client’s tech stack.

The dashboard is:

  • ✅ Easy to use
  • 🎯 Tailored for energy modelers
  • 🌐 Fully open-source and hosted on GitHub

Working with Dr. Masanet’s lab, we focused on the most meaningful filters and visualizations. We chose boxplots because they:

  1. Show typical value ranges
  2. Highlight clusters and patterns
  3. Reveal outliers quickly

With these visuals, analysts can move from question to insight — no spreadsheets required.

A Real Example

Erin Uses the Dashboard

Let’s revisit Erin’s motor replacement scenario.
She filters the dashboard for:

  • Texas
  • Motor recommendations
  • Time period: 1994–2024
  • All sectors (since motors are widely used)

What the Dashboard Reveals:

  • Electricity Savings:
    Plants that implemented upgrades saved between 20,000 and 85,000 kWh/year.

  • Investment & Payback:
    Implementers often faced higher upfront costs and longer payback periods, but still moved forward — showing confidence in long-term benefits.

  • Emissions Avoided:
    Each implementation avoided 2,500–14,000 kg of CO₂ annually, equivalent to driving 6,000–35,000 miles in a typical car.
    > (Note: Future iterations will also quantify NOₓ and SO₂)

  • Adoption Rates:
    Motor upgrades were implemented far more often than rejected, suggesting that barriers to adoption are relatively low.

In just a few minutes, Erin now has strong evidence to recommend further policy exploration around efficient motor upgrades.

What’s Next?

This dashboard is just the beginning. Future enhancements could include:

  • Extending emissions data to cover pre-1990
  • Adding NOₓ and SO₂ estimates
  • Supporting technology shift analysis over time
  • Moving from Excel to a database with API access

These improvements would make the tool even more powerful and easier to scale across states and industries.
